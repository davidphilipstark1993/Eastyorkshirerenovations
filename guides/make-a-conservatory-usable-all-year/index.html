How to make a conservatory usable all year round.

In short: A conservatory that is too hot in summer and too cold in winter almost always has the same cause: a fully glazed or thin polycarbonate roof. Replacing or insulating the roof makes the biggest difference, after which glazing, flooring and heating turn it into a room you can use as a living or dining room.

How to make a conservatory usable all year round

There's no single fix that does everything, but a handful of changes consistently make the biggest difference to year-round usability.

Priority order

Start with the roof — this addresses both summer overheating and winter cold more than any other single change. Floor insulation and heating come next, followed by upgrading doors and windows if they're original to an older conservatory. Electrics and lighting matter more for how the room gets used day-to-day than for temperature control.

Don't need to do it all at once

A roof-only replacement is a sensible first step if budget is tight, since it addresses the biggest single issue. A fuller transformation can follow later if needed, though doing more of the work in one project is usually more cost-effective than staging it over several separate jobs.

Why is my conservatory too hot in summer?

It's almost always the roof. A fully glazed or polycarbonate roof acts like a greenhouse in direct sun, and no amount of blinds or fans fully solves that on their own.

The main cause

A glass or polycarbonate roof lets solar heat straight through, and with nowhere for that heat to escape, the room quickly climbs well above outside temperature on a sunny day. Older single-glazed or thin polycarbonate roofs are the worst offenders.

What actually fixes it

Replacing the roof with an insulated tiled or panel system, or a high-performance solar-control glazed roof, is the single biggest improvement you can make — see our guide on insulated conservatory roofs. Ventilation upgrades, solar control film and external blinds can help at the margins, but they're managing the symptom rather than fixing the cause.

Why is my conservatory too cold in winter?

The same roof that lets your conservatory overheat in summer is usually why it's freezing in winter — a fully glazed or thin polycarbonate roof loses heat fast.

Where the heat is escaping

Roof, floor and older single-glazed windows or doors are the three main culprits. A thin or ageing roof loses heat quickly, an uninsulated floor slab pulls warmth down and out, and older sliding patio doors are rarely as well sealed as modern equivalents.

What to prioritise

Roof insulation makes the biggest single difference, followed by floor insulation if you're doing a fuller transformation. Upgrading doors and windows to modern, well-sealed units helps too, particularly if the existing ones are original to an older conservatory.

Turning a conservatory into a living room

This is one of the most common conservatory transformation requests we get — here's what it typically involves.

What changes

A roof upgrade is the starting point, since a living room needs to be comfortable in both summer and winter. Underfloor heating or radiators, proper flooring (carpet or engineered wood rather than a hard conservatory-style floor), plastered walls and decorating bring the room up to the same standard as the rest of the house.

Doors and layout

Upgrading old sliding patio doors to bi-fold or French doors is a popular addition, both for the look and for better insulation and sealing than older units.

Turning a conservatory into a dining room

A conservatory dining room needs to work for entertaining in the evening as much as in bright daylight, which changes a few priorities compared with a living room conversion.

Lighting and atmosphere

Good pendant or spotlight lighting matters more here than in a daytime-only space, since a dining room gets used through the evening. A solid or hybrid roof also helps avoid the glare and heat that make dining uncomfortable on sunny days.

Flooring and finish

Durable, easy-to-clean flooring suited to regular use around a dining table — tiling or engineered wood are common choices — alongside proper heating so the room's comfortable for long dinners in winter.

Frequently asked questions

How to make a conservatory usable all year round: FAQs.

What's the minimum I need to do to use my conservatory year-round?

For most conservatories, a proper insulated roof replacement makes the biggest single difference and is a reasonable starting point if you can't do a full transformation straight away.

Will blinds alone fix a hot conservatory?

They help reduce direct glare and some heat gain, but they don't address the underlying issue of heat building up under a fully glazed roof. A roof upgrade makes the biggest difference.

Is a roof replacement a big project?

It's usually one of the quicker conservatory transformation options, often completed in 1-2 weeks for a straightforward roof-only replacement.

Can I just add a heater instead of upgrading the roof?

You can, but you'll be heating a room that loses warmth quickly, which costs more to run long-term than fixing the insulation properly.

Does floor insulation make a noticeable difference?

Yes, particularly on an older conservatory with a thin, uninsulated slab — it's a common part of a fuller conservatory transformation.

Do I need building regulations approval to use my conservatory as a living room?

It depends on the finished specification — if the transformation removes the conservatory's thermal separation from the house (for example, opening it fully into a hallway with shared heating), building regulations are more likely to apply.

Do I need to replace the roof for a dining room conversion?

It's strongly recommended, since a fully glazed roof makes a dining room uncomfortable in both bright sun and winter cold.
